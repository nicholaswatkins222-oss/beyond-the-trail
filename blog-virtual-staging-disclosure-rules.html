AI & Virtual Staging Disclosure Rules: What Reno Realtors Need to Know in 2026

NAR updated its virtual staging rules late last year. California's AB 723 just took effect. Here's what every Northern Nevada listing agent should be doing — before the first MLS upload of 2026.

May 2026 Josh Young · Beyond the Trail Media 6 min read

Virtual staging has gone from a clever trick to a default tool on vacant listings. But the rules around it just changed. In October 2025, NAR clarified its guidance on AI-edited and virtually staged photos. In January 2026, California's AB 723 went into effect, making undisclosed AI alterations a misdemeanor for any listing visible to California buyers.

Nevada-specific legislation hasn't passed yet, but the NNRMLS and Greater Las Vegas MLS already follow NAR's "clearly and conspicuously" standard. If you're a Reno realtor and you're not disclosing virtual staging on the image itself, you're a complaint away from a problem. Here's what changed and how to stay clean.

What the New Rules Actually Say

NAR's October 2025 update sharpened a guidance that had been around for years. The key phrase: virtually staged or AI-altered photos must be disclosed "clearly and conspicuously" — meaning on the image itself, not buried in the property description.

  • Caption-only disclosure no longer counts. If a buyer can see the photo without seeing the disclosure, it's not "conspicuous."
  • Every altered image needs its own disclosure. One generic "some photos virtually staged" line in the description isn't enough.
  • The disclosure should describe the alteration. "Virtually staged" is fine. "Virtually decluttered" or "AI-enhanced sky" is better.

💡 The safest formula: an overlay label in the lower-left or upper-right of the image that reads "Virtually Staged" in a clearly legible font. The label needs to survive being downsized to a Zillow thumbnail.

California AB 723 — Why It Matters in Reno

California's AB 723 makes failure to disclose AI-altered real estate photos a misdemeanor in California. If you list a Reno property and a California-based buyer sees the listing on Zillow, Realtor.com, or Redfin — and the photos are altered without disclosure — you've now got a potential complaint vector in another state.

About 1 in 3 Reno-area buyers in 2025 came from California, according to data tracked by Reno-Sparks Association of Realtors. That's a lot of out-of-state eyes on every listing. Even if Nevada doesn't pass its own AB 723 equivalent, the practical exposure is real.

What's Still Allowed (and What Isn't)

Virtual staging itself is not the problem. The issue is the gap between what the photo shows and what the property actually is. Here's the rough line:

Allowed (with disclosure):

  • Adding furniture to an empty room
  • Decluttering — removing visible personal items, mail, kids' toys
  • Touch-up edits on stains, scuffs, or temporary blemishes
  • Sky replacement on exterior shots (overcast → blue sky), if disclosed
  • Lawn greening in fall/winter shots

Not allowed — even with disclosure:

  • Structural changes — removing or adding walls, windows, doors
  • Removing permanent defects — cracks in the foundation, water damage, sagging ceilings
  • Hiding power lines, neighbors' structures, or visible HOA violations
  • Adding fake views through windows (mountains, water, downtown)
  • Misrepresenting square footage via fish-eye or extreme wide-angle distortion

Penalties Reno Realtors Should Know About

Three layers of risk stack on top of each other for non-disclosed AI staging:

  • MLS fines — NNRMLS rules allow per-violation fines, typically $100–$500, escalating with repeat offenses.
  • Code of Ethics complaints — Article 12 of the NAR Code of Ethics requires "true picture" in advertising. A complaint can result in mandatory ethics training, fines up to $15,000, and public sanctions.
  • State licensing action — Nevada Real Estate Division can investigate misrepresentation complaints separately, with penalties up to license suspension.

💡 Beyond the formal penalties, the bigger risk is a buyer or buyer's agent finding the discrepancy after escrow opens. That's the kind of story that makes the local Facebook real estate groups, and the kind of reputation damage that's hard to undo.

The 4-Step Pre-Upload Checklist

Before any listing photo goes on MLS, run this quick check:

  • 1. Label every altered image on the image. An overlay in the corner that reads "Virtually Staged" — visible at thumbnail size.
  • 2. Keep the original. If a complaint comes in, you need to show the unaltered shot. Save both.
  • 3. Match the listing description. If the description says "fully furnished," and the photo shows AI-staged furniture, you've got a problem. Be explicit: "shown virtually staged, sold unfurnished."
  • 4. Document who did the edits and when. Photographer name, date, software used. This protects you if anything is ever challenged.

What This Means for Working with a Photographer

If you're hiring a photographer, ask them upfront: are they doing any AI alterations beyond standard HDR processing and color correction? Standard editing — exposure blending, white balance, perspective correction — doesn't count as "altered" under any of these rules. AI sky replacement, virtual staging, virtual decluttering all do.
